1  What it is, goals and examples

A prognostic meta-analysis pools studies that forecast a future outcome. The question is not whether a treatment works. It is what will happen to people who are already in a given health state, and how much knowing one more thing about them today changes what we should expect tomorrow.

That difference, from effect of an intervention to forecast of an outcome, decides everything else: which studies are eligible, which number is extracted from each one, which scale it is pooled on, and which sentence may be written in the discussion. Let us start by looking at what such a review produces, and only then name the parts.

Two published data sets carry every example in this book. The first is the review by (1) of HER2 as a prognostic factor in endometrial cancer, twenty studies with a hazard ratio each. The second is the EuroSCORE II model for death after cardiac surgery, its development study and twenty-two external validations, assembled by (2). Both ship with the book in data/, and nothing in these pages is drawn by hand.

1.1 What a prognostic meta-analysis is

A prognosis study follows people forward. It starts at a startpoint, a moment that is the same for everyone entering the study, such as the day of diagnosis or the day of surgery. It then waits for a fixed prediction horizon, thirty days or five years, and records whether the outcome happened.

Those two words are the whole design. Imagine two studies of the same cancer, both reporting a hazard ratio for the same marker. One starts the clock at diagnosis, the other at the end of first-line treatment. They are not estimating the same quantity, and pooling them produces a number that describes neither. The Cochrane handbook makes the longitudinal relationship between a startpoint and a later outcome the defining feature of all prognosis research (3).

Within that frame, the mechanics are the mechanics of any meta-analysis. We take one estimate per study, with its standard error, transform it to a scale on which it is approximately normal, and pool it by inverse variance. Let us do that with the twenty HER2 studies.

her2$TE   <- log(her2$HR)
her2$seTE <- (log(her2$HR.975) - log(her2$HR.025)) / 3.92

m_her2 <- metagen(TE, seTE, studlab = Study, data = her2, sm = "HR",
                  method.tau = "REML", method.random.ci = "HK",
                  prediction = TRUE)

forest(m_her2, layout = "RevMan5", common = FALSE,
       leftcols = c("studlab", "N", "effect.ci"),
       leftlabs = c("Study", "n", "HR [95% CI]"),
       prediction = TRUE, col.diamond = "black")

The plot looks like every forest plot we have ever drawn, and that is the point worth making early. What makes this a prognosis review is not the arithmetic. It is what the rows mean.

c(HR = exp(m_her2$TE.random),
  lower = exp(m_her2$lower.random),
  upper = exp(m_her2$upper.random),
  tau2 = m_her2$tau2)
        HR      lower      upper       tau2 
2.01331288 1.59143476 2.54702791 0.08077795

We see that the summary hazard ratio is about 2.0, with a 95% confidence interval running from roughly 1.6 to 2.6. Patients whose tumour overexpresses HER2 die at about twice the rate of those whose tumour does not. No treatment was given, no arms were compared, and nothing here says that HER2 causes the deaths. The marker earns its place by predicting, not by explaining.

Note

The scale matters more than it does in an intervention review, because prognosis estimates arrive in more flavours. Hazard ratios are pooled as logs, proportions as logits, c-statistics as logits, and the observed-to-expected ratio as a log. Chapter 4 gives the full table; for now it is enough to know that every estimate is transformed before it is pooled, and transformed back before it is read.

1.2 Three types, three questions

The handbook splits prognosis research into three types, following the PROGRESS framework (4). Every decision in the rest of this book depends on which of the three the review is, so let us meet them with the data in front of us.

Overall prognosis asks what happens on average. It pools absolute risks, not comparisons. From the EuroSCORE II cohorts we can ask the plainest possible question: how often do patients die within thirty days of cardiac surgery?

m_risk <- metaprop(event = round(Po * n), n = n, studlab = Study, data = euro,
                   sm = "PLOGIT", method = "Inverse", method.tau = "REML",
                   prediction = TRUE)

round(c(risk = plogis(m_risk$TE.random),
        lower = plogis(m_risk$lower.random),
        upper = plogis(m_risk$upper.random)), 4)
  risk  lower  upper 
0.0391 0.0317 0.0482

About four deaths per hundred operations, with a narrow interval around it. That number has no comparator in it at all, which is exactly what makes it an overall prognosis estimate.

Prognostic factor research asks whether knowing one characteristic changes the forecast. The HER2 analysis above is a factor review: one variable, measured at the startpoint, and its association with a later outcome. The estimate of interest is usually the adjusted one, because a factor only earns its keep if it adds something to the variables a clinician already has.

Prediction model research asks how well a whole multivariable model forecasts for one individual. EuroSCORE II is such a model. Its validations do not report a hazard ratio at all; they report how well the model separated survivors from deaths, and how close its predicted risks were to the observed ones.

euro$cl <- log(euro$c.index / (1 - euro$c.index))
se_delta <- euro$se.c.index / (euro$c.index * (1 - euro$c.index))
se_ci <- (log(euro$c.index.95CIu / (1 - euro$c.index.95CIu)) -
          log(euro$c.index.95CIl / (1 - euro$c.index.95CIl))) / 3.92
euro$secl <- ifelse(is.na(se_delta), se_ci, se_delta)

m_c <- metagen(cl, secl, studlab = Study, data = euro,
               method.tau = "REML", method.random.ci = "HK",
               prediction = TRUE)

round(plogis(c(c_statistic = m_c$TE.random,
               lower = m_c$lower.random,
               upper = m_c$upper.random)), 3)
c_statistic       lower       upper 
      0.794       0.771       0.816

A summary c-statistic of 0.79. The Cochrane handbook reports 0.79 with a confidence interval of 0.77 to 0.81 for this same set of validations (5), so a dozen lines of code have reproduced a published result.

A fourth type exists, predictors of treatment effect, which asks whether a characteristic changes how well a treatment works. That is a question about treatment-covariate interaction, and the handbook names it in Chapter 1 and then leaves it outside its scope (4). This book does the same.

Important

One review, one type. The three types have different eligibility criteria, different extraction forms, different appraisal tools and different reporting guidelines. A protocol that has not decided which type it is cannot specify any of them, which is why Chapter 2 starts with this decision and not with a search string.

1.3 What it never answers

A prognosis review forecasts. It does not explain, and it does not recommend a treatment.

The distinction is easy to state and easy to violate in a discussion section. Our HER2 analysis found a summary hazard ratio of about 2.0. It is tempting to write that HER2 overexpression worsens survival, and that blocking it should therefore improve survival. The first clause is a causal claim the design cannot support. The second is a treatment claim that needs a randomised trial.

Imagine a marker that is measured only in the sickest patients, because the test is ordered when a clinician is already worried. It will predict death beautifully and cause none of it. The forecast is correct and useful. The causal reading is wrong.

What a prognosis review may say is narrower and still valuable: in people like these, at this startpoint, over this horizon, those with the factor fare worse, by about this much, in most settings. Chapter 5 returns to the sentences that stay inside that boundary and the ones that stray outside it.

1.4 Where the data comes from

Prognosis studies are observational, and the handbook ranks the designs that feed them (3).

The prospective cohort is the ideal. The startpoint is defined in advance, the predictors are measured before the outcome, and the follow-up is planned. Retrospective cohorts are common and usable; the data were recorded for some other purpose, so the startpoint is reconstructed rather than designed.

Arms of randomised trials are prospective cohorts with a randomised startpoint. They give clean outcomes and a narrow case mix, which is a strength for measurement and a weakness for generalisation. Nested case-control and case-cohort designs sample efficiently within an existing cohort, and their estimates are relative ones; absolute risks require reweighting back to the source cohort before they mean anything.

Finally, routine data, meaning electronic health records and registries, increasingly carries prognosis research. The case mix is the real one, which is the point, and the measurement is whatever clinical practice happened to record, which is the cost.

knitr::kable(data.frame(
  Example = c("HER2 in endometrial cancer", "EuroSCORE II"),
  Type = c("Prognostic factor", "Prediction model"),
  Studies = c(nrow(her2), nrow(euro)),
  Participants = c(sum(her2$N), sum(euro$n)),
  Startpoint = c("Diagnosis", "Cardiac surgery"),
  Horizon = c("Survival", "30 days")
))

We see that the two reviews differ in every column, and the one that matters most is the last. A thirty-day horizon and a survival curve are not interchangeable, and the pooled estimate inherits the horizon of the studies that produced it.

1.5 The measures you will meet

Each type brings its own statistics, and reading them is most of the work of Chapter 4. Here we only name them, so that the examples in the next chapters are not a surprise.

Overall prognosis is reported as a cumulative incidence, the probability of the outcome by a time point, or as an incidence rate, events per person-time. Prognostic factors are reported as a hazard ratio for time-to-event outcomes, or a risk or odds ratio for a binary outcome by a fixed time.

Prediction models are reported with four kinds of number. Calibration asks whether predicted risks match observed ones, and its headline summary is the observed-to-expected ratio, written O:E. Discrimination asks whether patients who had the outcome received higher predictions than those who did not, and is summarised by the c-statistic, which equals the area under the ROC curve. Overall fit combines the two, and clinical utility asks whether using the model to make decisions does more good than harm.

Let us look at the calibration of EuroSCORE II, because it carries the lesson of the whole chapter.

euro$loe   <- log(euro$Po / euro$Pe)
euro$seloe <- sqrt((1 - euro$Po) / (euro$n * euro$Po))

m_oe <- metagen(loe, seloe, studlab = Study, data = euro, sm = "OE",
                method.tau = "REML", method.random.ci = "HK",
                prediction = TRUE)

forest(m_oe, layout = "RevMan5", common = FALSE,
       leftcols = c("studlab", "n", "effect.ci"),
       leftlabs = c("Study", "n", "O:E [95% CI]"),
       prediction = TRUE, ref = 1, col.diamond = "black")
round(exp(c(OE = m_oe$TE.random,
            lower = m_oe$lower.random,
            upper = m_oe$upper.random,
            PI.lower = m_oe$lower.predict,
            PI.upper = m_oe$upper.predict)), 2)
      OE    lower    upper PI.lower PI.upper 
    1.12     0.92     1.37     0.46     2.76

The summary O:E is about 1.1, and perfect calibration sits inside its confidence interval. On average the model slightly underestimates risk. Now read the last two numbers. The 95% prediction interval is far wider, which says that in a new hospital the model may predict considerably more deaths than occur, or considerably fewer.

That gap between a reassuring average and an alarming range is the single most important habit this book will try to build. The handbook makes the same point with the same data (5).

1.6 What the published examples look like

Both of our examples are ordinary published reviews, and it is worth seeing what each one had to decide.

(1) asked whether hormone receptors predict survival in endometrial cancer. The team had to standardise the direction of every hazard ratio, because studies compare positive against negative in whichever order suits their narrative, and they had to decide what to do with studies that reported only an unadjusted estimate. Twenty studies with usable numbers survived that process.

(2) asked how EuroSCORE II performs outside the data it was built on. The team had to find validations that reported enough to compute both discrimination and calibration, which is a stricter filter than it sounds: a validation that prints only a c-statistic can enter the discrimination analysis and nothing else.

The handbook adds a third kind of example that every reviewer should read once. In the living review of covid-19 prediction models by Wynants and colleagues, almost every model identified was judged at high risk of bias (5). A review can be executed perfectly and still conclude that the evidence cannot be used. That is a result, and Chapter 5 shows how to report it.

1.7 Should you use it?

Let us end where a project actually starts, with three questions in order.

Is the question a prognosis question? If the answer names a treatment and a comparator, it is an intervention review. If it names a test and a target condition at the same moment, it is a diagnostic accuracy review. If it names a population, a startpoint, a horizon and an outcome, it belongs here.

Which of the three types is it? Average outcome, one factor, or a whole model. The rest of the book branches on this answer.

Do the published studies report what the method needs? This is the viability question, and it is the subject of Chapter 2. A hazard ratio without a confidence interval, a c-statistic without a sample size, a model validation that reports only a p-value: each of these costs a study, and enough of them cost the review.

Test your knowledge!

  1. A review pools the proportion of patients still unable to work twelve months after a back injury. Which of the three types is it?
  2. Why is the adjusted estimate usually of more interest than the crude one in a prognostic factor review?
  3. A model validation reports a c-statistic of 0.82 and an O:E of 1.9. What is good about the model, and what is wrong with it?
  4. The summary O:E of a model is close to 1 but its 95% prediction interval runs from 0.46 to 2.76. A hospital asks whether it may use the model. What do you tell them?

1.8 Summary

  • A prognosis review forecasts a future outcome from a defined startpoint over a defined prediction horizon. Those two anchors decide which studies answer the same question.
  • Prognosis research comes in three types: overall prognosis, prognostic factors and prediction models. The type decides eligibility, extraction, appraisal and reporting, so it is settled before anything else.
  • The arithmetic is standard inverse-variance meta-analysis. What is specific to prognosis is the scale each measure is pooled on, and the meaning of the rows.
  • Prediction is not explanation. A factor earns its place by forecasting, and no causal or treatment claim follows from a prognosis review.
  • An average is not enough. The prediction interval tells us what to expect in a new setting, and for EuroSCORE II it is wide where the average looked calm.
